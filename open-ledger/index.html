03INVESTING / FOUNDATIONS

A price is visible.
Its meaning takes work.

A quoted share price is a number at a moment in time. By itself, it does not explain what the company owns, how it earns money or what the future might hold.

An estimate of value goes further: it depends on a method and assumptions. Reading those assumptions matters as much as reading the final number. A difference between price and estimated value is not a prediction of what happens next.

Open the glossary: what is a share?

A share is a unit of ownership in a company. The rights it carries depend on its class. A market price can change, and ownership involves risk. Price, ownership rights and the economics of the business are connected ideas, but they are not interchangeable.

04PORTFOLIO / A THINKING FRAMEWORK

The holdings are only
part of the picture.

A portfolio can be read as a set of connected decisions. This framework explains the questions; it does not suggest what anyone should hold.

01 / PURPOSEWhat is it for?

A goal gives the discussion context. Different goals may lead to different questions.

02 / TIMEWhen is it needed?

A possible withdrawal date and an asset’s holding period are distinct ideas.

03 / RISKWhat could change?

Consider uncertainty, concentration and the possibility of losses together.

04 / COSTWhat is paid?

Fees, dealing costs and taxes are separate concepts. Ask what a quoted charge includes.

Open the glossary: diversification

Diversification spreads exposure across different holdings or sources of risk. How those exposures behave together matters; a longer list alone does not explain the result. Diversification cannot remove every risk or guarantee a return.

Imagine a small workshop. During a single period, it sells £100 of goods and incurs £70 of expenses. In this deliberately simplified example, it records £30 of profit.

There is a second story running alongside that first one. Customers do not all pay at the moment the workshop records a sale. The workshop’s expenses, meanwhile, have already been paid. To understand the cash balance, we need to follow those payments.

01 / One business, different questions.

Revenue asks what sales were recognised. Profit asks what remains after the relevant expenses. Cash movement asks what money actually came in and went out. Each is useful, but the questions are different.

For this lesson, every expense is recognised and paid in the same period. There are no taxes, borrowing, capital purchases or other transactions. Those restrictions are doing real work: they allow one timing difference to stand out.

02 / Follow the customer payments.

At the lab’s starting setting, customers pay £60. Another £40 is still owed. That unpaid amount is a receivable: a claim to a customer payment, rather than cash already collected.[1]

01 / RECEIVABLES

Money owed by customers after a recognised sale. An expectation of collection is different from cash in the bank; real research asks about timing and collectability.

The cash arithmetic is now straightforward: £60 received minus £70 paid is a £10 decrease. The profit calculation remains £100 revenue minus £70 expenses, or £30.

Timing changes cash.
It does not rewrite this period’s sales.

The £40 gap between £30 profit and a £10 cash decrease matches the increase in customer receivables. In this example, that is the whole bridge between the two measures.

04 / What this does—and does not—mean.

Collecting another £10 in the same period reduces receivables by £10 and improves cash movement by £10. In this model it does not change revenue or profit. At £70 collected, cash movement is zero. At £100 collected, it is positive £30.[2]

02 / THE THRESHOLD

The £70 point follows directly from £70 of cash expenses. It is a feature of this invented example, not a universal benchmark for company health.

None of that establishes whether an actual business is attractive, safe or fairly priced. A receivable may be collected late, disputed or not collected at all. Other expenses may not involve current cash payments. Investing and financing transactions may also change cash.

A simplified model is useful because its assumptions are narrow and visible. It becomes misleading if we forget those boundaries.

05 / Ask what comes next.

The next research step is to seek evidence, not to turn the example into a recommendation.

  1. When are customers expected to pay, and what evidence supports that expectation?
  2. How have receivables changed alongside sales across comparable periods?
  3. Which other items reconcile profit to operating cash flow in the actual accounts?
  4. What does management explain, and what remains uncertain after reading the primary statements?

06MARKETS / CONTEXT BEFORE CONCLUSIONS

A headline is the start.
The mechanism is the lesson.

Separate three things when you read market coverage: what happened, how it might work and what remains uncertain. A single headline rarely supplies all three.

Try the exercise: “Interest rates have changed.”

First ask which rate changed and who set it. Then ask how it could affect borrowing costs or valuation assumptions. Finally, separate those possible channels from any claim about the next market move. This is a reading exercise, not current news or a forecast.

Try another: observation or interpretation?

“The company reported a lower cash balance” describes an observation, if supported by its accounts. “Customers are struggling to pay” is an interpretation that needs separate evidence. Keep the two sentences apart until the connection is established.
