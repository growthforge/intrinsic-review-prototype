01 / Observe before you explain.

Imagine a small workshop. We are given four monthly output counts: 80, 92, 88 and 100 units. That is the entire source for this exercise. Every number has been invented.

The final count is higher than the first. We can calculate the change. We can also see that output did not increase in every month. Those observations stay close to the information we actually have.

Now consider a different sentence: “Demand is getting stronger.” It might be true. It might also be a story that happens to fit the numbers. The workshop could have changed its opening hours, cleared a backlog or installed a new machine. We have not been told.

01 / What counts as evidence?

Here, “evidence” means a statement directly supported by the stipulated example. In real research, you would also assess whether the original source is reliable, complete and correctly measured.

02 / Name the assumption.

Suppose we extend the series. We could hold output at 100 units, increase it by 10% each month, or reduce it by 10% each month. The starting point stays the same; the assumption changes.

None of those paths becomes evidence simply because we draw a neat line. Each path answers a conditional question: what would follow if this assumption held?

04 / Ask what is missing.

Good research does not end with a confident sentence. It ends with a clear account of what supports that sentence—and what could change it.

For our workshop, the next step is to find information that can distinguish between explanations. A longer time series would add context. Production hours, orders and capacity might help us understand the mechanism. We should also ask whether “units” meant the same thing in every period.

  1. What does the original source actually measure?
  2. Which part of the explanation did I add myself?
  3. What new evidence would change my mind?
03 / A habit to keep

Write “I don’t know yet” beside the missing information. It is a useful research task, not a flaw to hide beneath a more precise-looking number.

A few useful starting points

Keep the questions going.

Investing / field note 01

First, ask what you own.

A share represents an ownership interest. A bond is a form of lending. A fund pools investments. Start by describing the claim you hold, before imagining what it might return.

Try a question

If a fictional fund holds shares in many businesses, what do you own directly? An interest in the fund. To understand the underlying exposure, you would inspect its holdings and governing documents.

Portfolio / field note 02

Different labels.
The same risk?

Two funds can own many of the same businesses. Counting fund names alone can miss shared exposure. Diversification depends on what sits underneath, and cannot eliminate every risk.

Try a question

Imagine two baskets each containing the same three fictional businesses. Combining the baskets changes the packaging, but does not add a new underlying business. What would you compare before calling them different?

Markets / field note 03

Give a headline
some context.

Ask which measure changed, over which period and compared with what. A direction is only part of the story. The source, units and starting point help you understand its scale.

Try a question

In a hypothetical example, an index moves from 80 to 100. That is a 25% increase. A fall from 100 back to 80 is 20%. The change in units is the same; the percentage uses a different starting value.
